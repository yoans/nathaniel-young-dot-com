Interactive exhibits · Des Moines Children's Museum

Exhibits where kids are the QA team

Contract engineering and design partner · Sagaciasoft LLC

The situation

A children's museum needed exhibits that actually work: a phone system kids can talk to each other on, a fire house where aiming a water turret registers a hit, and a construction house where children route pipes through a wall.

Museum exhibits are a brutal engineering environment. The users are children, so every interaction is an attack surface. The exhibit runs all day, every day, for years, with no engineer on site. Anything confusing gets abandoned in ten seconds by a six year old.

The risk

The expensive commitments come early. Cutting PVC, ordering sensors, fabricating enclosures, and painting finished millwork all cost real money and cannot be undone. The museum needed to know an exhibit would be fun and would work before anyone committed materials to it.

Fun is hard to specify on paper. For the construction house, the open questions were whether a pipe routing puzzle is enjoyable, whether a given wall layout is solvable, how busy the lighting would look, and how much room a child needs to invent a route. A drawing cannot answer those.

What got proven first

A playable design tool, before materials. The Pipe Puzzle Designer models the construction house wall in a browser. The client can try layouts, invent routes, watch spills, and see whether a configuration is solvable and how active the lighting gets. No install, no account, no engineer in the room. Design conversations moved from opinions about a drawing to reactions to a thing just played. Total cost of being wrong: a browser refresh.

Working hit detection on a physical prototype. For the Fire House, the museum's water turret prototype got miss-left, hit, and miss-right detection so the interaction could be evaluated for real. Functional questions get answered early. Appearance stays with the museum's strengths.

Phone stations built for experience validation before finish work. Confirming how the phones feel comes before irreversible paint and millwork.

What I was responsible for

  • Exhibit concept options. Recommendations used to narrow scope, budget, and sequencing.
  • Phone system, end to end. Technical design, control software, multi-station audio, build guides, enclosure and faceplate layout, wiring harness, provisioning, and test runbooks.
  • Fire House sensing. Hit and miss detection on the museum's water turret prototype.
  • Construction House. Phase 1 plumbing proposal plus the browser design tool used before fabrication.
  • Delivery management. Sequencing, risk documentation, hardware purchasing and expense tracking, written status the museum can circulate.

Competencies: embedded Linux and single-board computers, multi-station audio, sensor integration, enclosure and harness design for public installations, interactive design for non-readers, browser-based simulation tools, hardware purchasing and project accounting, client-facing technical writing.

What the client owns

The museum keeps the design tool for future layouts. They keep the build guides, enclosure and harness specs, and the setup and troubleshooting runbooks so staff can provision, test, and repair stations. Fabrication, paint, and finish stay in-house.

The shape of this work

An organization with a vivid idea, real funding, real physical stakes, and nobody on staff who can make electronics behave. Engineering risk gets retired early and cheaply, in tools the client can operate, so the expensive irreversible work happens only after the answers are in.
